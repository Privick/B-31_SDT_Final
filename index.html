Systems Thinking & Human-Centred Design · Final Project · Academic Group 31

One Nation, One Standard

Making a school mark mean the same thing everywhere in India — the project story and both working prototypes, combined in one file.

Prototype 1 · physical / digital

The Calibration Drill

HMW 1: How might we make evaluators in every state award marks to the same standard, when generosity is trained, not personal?
  • Mark a real Class XII script blind, out of 20
  • See the panel's marks, the reasoning, and your drift against a tolerance band
  • Mark a second script and measure whether your drift narrowed
  • Facilitator view aggregates every marker to show the group spread
Prototype 2 · digital

The Dual Marksheet

HMW 2: How might we make a mark from any board mean the same thing across India, without asking any state to give up its syllabus or language?
  • Enter a candidate's board marks and choose the examining board
  • The raw mark is never altered - one percentile column is added
  • A plain-language line explains what the percentile means
  • Switch boards to see the same mark become a different percentile
Why these two

They attack different points

The drill stops the gap from being created. The marksheet stops the existing gap from doing damage. Neither depends on the other.

They need no new law

Both can start inside a single board, voluntarily. Syllabus, language and brand stay untouched - the one concession our interviews said boards would make.

They were revised on feedback

The drill became a private mirror and gained a tolerance band; the marksheet gained a plain-language line and subject-wise percentiles.

Scripts, panel marks and distribution parameters are illustrative and built for this prototype. A live system would use each board's anonymised distributions supplied to PARAKH, anchored on a common test. Full method and sources are on the Project Story tab.

The problem

Same effort, same country — different marks

India has 69 school examination boards teaching one shared NCERT textbook, but no common marking standard. A capable student assessed by a stricter-marking board needs a school-leaving score that means the same thing nationally, because a number assigned at seventeen follows him into every selection that matters. The gap is created once, at age 17, then charged again at 21 — CUET closes it temporarily at eighteen, but MBA and other shortlists (which weight Class 10 and 12 marks at 20–40% of the score) reopen it years later, long after either student could act on it.

The system

Two reinforcing loops keep the gap regenerating, and a third only hides it:

R1 · Results race — A board is judged publicly on its pass percentage. Generosity lifts that number, the number becomes the benchmark, and every other board feels the same pull. (pressure → generosity → pass % → pressure)
R2 · Trust erosion — Generosity destroys comparability, so selectors trust board marks less. Boards respond by inflating further to protect their own students. (generosity ↑ → comparability ↓ → trust ↓ → generosity ↑)
B1 · Workaround — Entrance exams partly correct the distortion, but treat the symptom: the school mark still carries weight years later.

Diagnosis: the system is unified everywhere except at the exact point where it is measured — and the people who pay for that have no seat where the norm is set.

Where we chose to act

Using Meadows' leverage-points framework, we deliberately acted on two mid-depth points rather than the deepest (paradigm — needs consent we can't prototype in a semester) or the shallowest (parameters — where states already act and nothing changes):

Leverage 1 · Information flows

Change what a mark tells the reader and every downstream decision changes — without touching a syllabus. → The Dual Marksheet

Leverage 2 · Goals

Shift a board's goal from "pass our students" to "issue a comparable credential" and R1 loses its fuel. → The Calibration Drill

What stakeholders told us

Four semi-structured interviews — a professor on a state board council, a principal with 27 years across Rajasthan, Kerala and CBSE, and two undergraduate students from different boards with the same CUET band — shaped both prototypes:

"Boards will concede a common standard. They will not concede their identity." — Professor & board member
"It feels like I inherited an advantage I did not earn." — Student, Kerala board
"Judging us on a number from when we were 17 is judging our geography." — Student, Rajasthan board

Three things changed our thinking: generosity is a trained norm (a design variable, not a character flaw); even the beneficiary calls it unfair (a legitimacy problem, not a grievance); and resistance is about identity and control — so leave syllabus, language and brand alone.

The Calibration Drill and Percentile Layer (Dual Marksheet) scored highest on impact relative to difficulty, and — critically — are independent: if one stalls politically, the other still delivers, and both can start inside a single board with no national agreement needed.

Evidence from testing

12markers tested (10 peers, 2 teaching staff)
11→4marks of spread on the same script
3.4→1.2standard deviation, before → after

What surprised us: markers disagreed on what a partial answer is worth, not on what was right; the drift card mattered more than the model answers; and two markers overcorrected — proving the target has to be a range, never a single number.

What changed on stakeholder feedback

Calibration Drill

Results are shown to the marker only, never the board — a mirror, not a monitor. The target became a tolerance band instead of one correct number, after markers in testing chased a single figure and overcorrected.

Dual Marksheet

A plain-language sentence was added after students asked "percentile of what?", and percentiles are reported subject-wise rather than as one aggregate figure.

Pattern across both: every objection was about control, not accuracy. Nobody argued marking is consistent today — they argued about who would hold the evidence that it isn't.

Rollout — one board, one subject, one cohort, then extend

Both are designed for voluntary adoption by a single board, needing no legislative change: the Calibration Drill is board-owned from month 3 (build kit with board panel) through month 12 (measure drift, extend subjects); the Dual Marksheet runs as a shadow marksheet before going live, using three years of anonymised past results equated against a common anchor supplied to PARAKH. Honest caveat: four interviews cannot represent 69 boards, and our own test used peers, not trained board evaluators — this is a hypothesis to be modelled next in Vensim, not a finished finding.
